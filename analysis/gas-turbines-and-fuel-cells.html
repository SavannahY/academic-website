INDEPENDENT INDUSTRY ANALYSIS · OCTOBER 25, 2026

Gas turbines & Bloom Energy: comparing the full cost of power

The decision depends on the complete system, the electricity it can deliver, and when that power becomes available.

Jane Yang · Energy infrastructure & project economics

My starting point

I evaluate onsite generation at the data center’s electrical boundary: net usable power, a defined reliability requirement, and a realistic energization date. A turbine equipment price and a fuel-cell service proposal can cover very different things. Comparing their headline dollars per kilowatt can hide the costs that determine the project.

My screening approach is to compare the complete installed systems first, then examine operating costs and the value of earlier deployment. A preferred technology should emerge from those assumptions, with the conditions that would change the decision stated explicitly.

Define the same project on both sides

What the public evidence establishes

The EIA / Sargent & Lundy capital-cost study for AEO2025 separates mechanical, electrical, civil, and indirect costs. Its reference projects are useful for understanding scope; their historical cost basis and utility-scale configurations need adjustment before application to a specific data center.

Bloom’s February 2026 Energy Server datasheet specifies net AC output, fuel pressure, and efficiency on stated bases. Those specifications help define an engineering comparison, while an installed project price and service obligations still require a proposal.

In its July 2025 Oracle announcement, Bloom described a 90-day deployment plan for selected sites. I treat that as a vendor statement about that collaboration. For another project, I would ask which prerequisites and milestones the proposed schedule includes.

Separate the electricity cost from the value of time

For a cost comparison, I would use discounted installed, fuel, maintenance, and replacement costs divided by discounted net electricity delivered, with the same study period and financing assumptions. Incentives belong in a separate scenario with eligibility and timing stated.

Scale makes small energy-cost differences consequential. As an illustration, a 100 MW facility operating at 90% annual utilization consumes 788,400 MWh a year. A 1 cent/kWh difference equals about $7.9 million annually, before discounting. This is arithmetic under those assumptions, not a project cost estimate.

I would assess earlier energization separately: how much useful computing capacity could actually operate earlier, for how long, and with what economic value? That value can justify a higher electricity cost, but it depends on the customer’s demand, facility readiness, and compute economics.

What would change the decision?

A comparison should identify the gas-price, installed-cost, service-cost, and schedule assumptions at which the preference changes. The next evidence I would seek is a scope-matched proposal, a documented delivery slot, a gas-service assessment, and a reliability design that accounts for outages. Those checks turn a technology narrative into a project decision.

Scope: a public comparison framework. It does not present a site-specific design, vendor quotation, or modeled cost ranking. Source documents retain their original dates.
